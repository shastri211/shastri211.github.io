<Section id="context" title="Context">

Customer churn is a standard prediction problem, and a standard way to overstate a result. A score is easy to produce. What a team can responsibly do with it depends on questions a leaderboard number does not answer. Is the probability calibrated? Are the reasons given for a score real or decorative? Does the data contain anything that could justify a recommendation?

RetainAI starts from those questions. It is a retention decision-support project whose first phase is deliberately narrow: validate the data, train and compare baselines honestly, score customers, explain each score, size the revenue exposed, and audit how the model behaves across groups. The only dataset is the public IBM Telco Customer Churn sample of 7,043 customers, which IBM documents as data for a fictional company.

</Section>

<Section id="objective" title="Objective">

Phase 1 had to produce a base that later phases could build on without having to revisit it.

- A data contract that fails loudly and reports known issues instead of hiding them.
- Baseline models compared under a protocol fixed in advance, with a rule for which one ships.
- Scores that mean what they say, checked by calibration, and reasons tied exactly to what the model computed.
- Every report labelled with what it cannot show.

</Section>

<Section id="approach" title="Approach">

The work was committed one stage at a time, each as its own commit: product direction and roadmap, a preserved exploratory analysis, a reproducible environment, then data validation, feature engineering, baseline models, risk scoring, explanations, value proxy, subgroup audit, tests and documentation.

<Section id="architecture" title="Architecture">

A pip-installable Python package, `retainai`, with one subpackage per stage: `data`, `features`, `models`, `scoring`, `explain`, `value` and `fairness`, and a small command-line interface. The source is about 2,500 lines, with about 1,000 lines of design documents beside it: a product requirements document, an architecture, a roadmap, a data strategy and a numbered decision log.

There is no service, database or user interface in this phase. The decision log records one Python package, no microservices and no queues for the planned later phases too, because no independent scaling or deployment need exists. The README adds that each later capability has to earn its place with evidence.

</Section>

<Section id="engineering" title="Engineering">

### Splitting data without leaking duplicates

The dataset contains duplicate customer rows. The duplicate groups all sit at a tenure of 1 month, where total charges equal monthly charges. Splits use `StratifiedGroupKFold` with the duplicate group as the group, so copies of one customer cannot land on both sides of a split. Cross-validation repeats 5 splits three times, giving 15 folds, on 5,634 development rows. The first fold of a separate grouped split is the 1,409-row holdout.

### Choosing the model by a rule

The report states the selection rule before it states the result. Gradient boosting scored best on cross-validated PR-AUC, ahead of logistic regression by 0.006. The standard deviation of that metric across folds was 0.020, so the benchmark did not clear one deviation and logistic regression shipped. Cross-validated ROC-AUC tells the same story: 0.840 for logistic regression, 0.841 for gradient boosting and 0.839 for the random forest.

### Thresholds and bands

The operating thresholds were fixed on development out-of-fold predictions before the holdout was read. The best-F1 threshold there was 0.29. Risk bands use cutoffs of 0.25 and 0.50, chosen from what a calibrated probability means, since 0.25 is about the base rate, and not tuned on outcomes. On the holdout, observed churn is 9.0 percent in LOW, 34.3 percent in MEDIUM and 70.2 percent in HIGH. The report says the bands are communication labels, not decision thresholds.

### Explanations that add up

For a logistic regression, the log-odds for a customer minus the log-odds of the average training profile equals the sum of each feature's weight times its distance from that average. Summing the encoded columns that come from one original feature gives that feature's contribution. The contributions add up exactly to what the model computed. Each explanation is labelled `MODEL_SIGNAL`, with a caveat that it is a pattern in the data and not a cause. Across the dataset, tenure moves the log-odds most, with a mean absolute contribution of 1.26, followed by total charges, contract type and monthly charges.

### Reproducibility

Runtime dependencies are pinned to exact versions. Reports record the dataset's SHA-256 after normalising line endings, and the model version. A slow test retrains on the real data and checks that the committed reports reproduce.

</Section>

<Section id="challenges" title="Challenges">

<Note label="Correcting my own Phase 0 findings, 6 Oct 2026 (fefe4ae)">

Checking the data properly in Phase 1 showed that three statements from the discovery phase were over-stated. Two-year contracts with fewer than 24 months of tenure are plausible, not inconsistent. The duplicate groups all sit at tenure 1, which explains them and makes them harmless to group. And a survival-based customer lifetime value cannot be validated on a single snapshot with an undefined churn horizon. The corrections are recorded as D-18 in the decision log instead of being edited out of the earlier documents.

</Note>

<Note label="A dataset that cannot support the obvious next step">

The data has no treatments, offers, costs, dates or outcomes after an intervention. That rules out validating uplift, temporal validation and a real customer lifetime value, which are the things a retention product ultimately needs. The decision log handles it by sequencing: uplift moves to Phase 7 and is gated on a data-strategy decision, because it cannot be validated on this dataset. The planned MVP runs on assumed effects with no causal claims, and the value figures stay labelled as proxies until then.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="trade-offs" title="Trade-offs">

<Decision
  title="Ship logistic regression"
  chose="The logistic regression, by a written rule that prefers the interpretable model unless a benchmark clearly beats it."
  over="Gradient boosting, which scored marginally higher on cross-validated PR-AUC."
  because="The gap was less than a third of one fold deviation, and the linear model's explanations are exact."
  cost="If a nonlinear model really is better, 7,043 rows are too few to show it, and the explanation method would have to change if the model did."
/>

<Decision
  title="Exact decomposition instead of an explanation library"
  chose="Weight times distance from the training mean, summed per original feature."
  over="A general-purpose attribution method."
  because="The contributions add up exactly to the model's own log-odds, so every explanation can be checked by arithmetic."
  cost="It only works for a linear model, so it is tied to the choice above."
/>

<Decision
  title="Keep gender and senior citizen out of the model"
  chose="Treat them as audit-only attributes and report model behaviour by group."
  over="Including them because they carry some signal."
  because="Adding both changed cross-validated ROC-AUC by 0.0002, well inside its deviation of 0.001, so they were not buying accuracy."
  cost="Excluding an attribute does not remove its proxies. The audit notes that tenure, partner and dependents may correlate with age."
/>

<Decision
  title="Fix thresholds before reading the holdout"
  chose="Choose operating thresholds on development out-of-fold predictions only."
  over="Tuning them on the holdout for the best-looking figure."
  because="A holdout used for tuning stops being a holdout."
  cost="The holdout is one fold of 1,409 customers, so its metrics are noisy and are reported without an interval."
/>

<Decision
  title="Bands from probability meaning, not from outcomes"
  chose="Cutoffs of 0.25 and 0.50, from the base rate and from what more likely than not means."
  over="Cutoffs tuned to maximise a metric."
  because="A band is a label for people, and a label tuned on outcomes would quietly encode the dataset's quirks."
  cost="The bands are unrelated to intervention capacity or cost, which the data cannot supply."
/>

<Section id="reflection" title="Reflection">

If I continued it today:

- **Put the tests in CI.** 142 tests run only on my machine. A workflow that runs them, and the slow reproducibility test on a schedule, is the cheapest credibility gain available.
- **Put intervals on the holdout.** The cross-validated figures have deviations; the holdout metrics are single numbers from one fold.
- **Say which evidence tier each later phase will claim.** The roadmap's MVP runs on assumed effects, and uplift waits for a data-strategy decision. Writing that tier onto every output, as the Phase 1 reports already do with their provenance label, keeps assumed effects from being read as measured ones.
- **Resolve the dataset's redistribution terms.** The sample is committed to the repository, and its terms and the repository's licence file are both unsettled.

</Section>
